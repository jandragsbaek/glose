Flow activities have rules and a structure that tell you what to do next and how well you are doing. A climber knows the next hold and feels at once whether it holds. A musician hears each note. These clear goals and quick feedback keep attention on the task and leave no room to wonder ([[Flow happens when challenge meets skill]]).

Many everyday tasks lack this, which is why they rarely produce flow. But we can add it ourselves: set a small, concrete goal and find a way to see results quickly. This is the trick behind [[Work can be transformed into flow]].

Feedback also teaches. Ahrens argues that [[Writing gives feedback on what you have understood]], and Brown shows that tests correct our sense of what we know ([[Calibration corrects judgments of learning]]). In both cases, quick and honest feedback makes the work more focused and more rewarding.
