I picked this up because I wanted to know if my own study habits were any good. Short answer: mostly not. For years I reread and highlighted, and it felt like work, so I assumed it worked. The book (written with the cognitive scientists Henry Roediger and Mark McDaniel) takes that feeling apart. [[Rereading creates an illusion of knowing]], and the strategies that feel slow and clumsy are often the ones that last.

The core is simple enough to fit on a card: test yourself, space it out, mix it up. [[Retrieval practice strengthens memory]] was the big one for me. I now close the book after a chapter and try to write down what I remember before I look again. That is close to what Ahrens asks for when he says to [[Write notes in your own words]], and I like that the two books meet there.

What stayed with me most is the idea that effort is not a sign that something is wrong. [[Desirable difficulties make learning stick]], and [[Mistakes are part of learning]], as long as I get feedback. I was also glad to see the authors say plainly that [[Learning styles are not supported by evidence]], since I had half believed in them.

The stories (pilots, a neurosurgeon, kids throwing beanbags) make it readable, though the middle chapters repeat themselves a bit.
