The famous bat-and-ball puzzle shows it: a quick answer pops up, it feels right, and many clever people give it without checking. Checking would have been easy. System 2 could have done the sum, but it did not bother. Kahneman calls this a law of least effort: we spend mental effort only when we must, and an intuitive answer that feels good enough is accepted as it is ([[System 1 is fast and System 2 is slow]]).

For me the practical lesson is to not rely on effort at the moment of decision. If the right action needs System 2 to wake up every time, it will often lose. That is the argument behind [[Environment design beats willpower]] and behind [[Rituals reduce the willpower that deep work needs]]. Kahneman also links this laziness to depleted self-control, but that part of the research is now in doubt ([[Ego depletion is a disputed account of self-control]]).
